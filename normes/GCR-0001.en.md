## Summary

This standard fixes how an RSA key is made. It changes nothing in RSA: a conforming key is an ordinary RSA key, accepted by all existing software. It fixes one thing only, generation: the two primes are built from a **seed**, by a computation described here in full, so that the same seed gives the same key on any machine and with any program written from this text.

Whoever holds a conforming key can therefore prove that it comes from the public computation and from nothing else: they make it again elsewhere, with another program, and compare. A faulty or malicious generator does not pass that test.

The aim is for African certification authorities to issue certificates whose keys were made by a generator mastered from end to end. These certificates are ordinary X.509 certificates: they serve every purpose a certificate serves (websites, mail, document and code signing, identity), and change nothing for those who read them.

This standard invents no computation. The construction of the primes is the Shawe-Taylor provable prime construction published in FIPS 186-4 (appendices B.3.2, C.6 and C.10). Its contribution is to make that construction mandatory, to describe it without cross-reference, to attach a generation record to it, and to define the checks that declare a key conforming.

## 1. Purpose and scope

1.1. This standard applies to the generation of RSA key pairs of 2048, 3072 and 4096 bits.

1.2. It is aimed first at the keys of certification authorities and the keys of the certificates they issue, and applies to any other RSA key.

1.3. It defines neither signature, nor encryption, nor certificate formats. Those remain as defined by RFC 8017 and the texts that depend on it.

1.4. It does not define the hardware source of randomness. It only fixes what the seed must be (section 5).

1.5. It does not address algorithms resistant to quantum computers. Section 11 states what follows from that.

## 2. Conventions

2.1. The words **MUST**, **MUST NOT**, **SHOULD**, **SHOULD NOT** and **MAY**, in capitals, have the meaning defined by RFC 2119 and RFC 8174. They translate the French words DOIT, NE DOIT PAS, DEVRAIT, NE DEVRAIT PAS and PEUT.

2.2. A requirement stated with MUST or MUST NOT can be verified by a check of section 10. A key that breaks a single one is non-conforming.

2.3. All numbers are non-negative integers. `⌈x⌉` rounds up to the next integer, `⌊x⌋` rounds down, `gcd` is the greatest common divisor, `lcm` the least common multiple, `a mod b` the remainder of `a` divided by `b`, `⊕` the bitwise exclusive or.

2.4. Converting a byte string to an integer, or back, is always done most significant byte first.

2.5. The names of the functions and variables of sections 6 and 7 are translated here for reading; they carry no normative weight. The field names of the generation record (section 8) are normative and are kept in French.

## 3. Definitions

3.1. **Seed**: secret byte string from which the whole key follows.

3.2. **Modulus**: the integer `n = p × q`, the public part of the key together with the exponent `e`.

3.3. **Replay**: making the key again from the seed and the parameters, and comparing the modulus obtained with the modulus declared.

3.4. **Generation record**: public document that accompanies the key (section 8).

3.5. **Independent implementation**: program written from this text, without reusing the code of another implementation, preferably in another language and by another person.

3.6. **Holder**: person or organisation responsible for the private key.

## 4. Parameters

4.1. The size of the modulus, written `nlen`, MUST be 2048, 3072 or 4096 bits.

4.2. A new key SHOULD have a modulus of at least 3072 bits. The 2048-bit size is allowed for compatibility with what exists.

4.3. The public exponent `e` MUST be 65537.

4.4. The hash function, written `H`, MUST be SHA-256, SHA-384 or SHA-512 (FIPS 180-4). Its output is `outlen` bits long: 256, 384 or 512.

4.5. The four parameters of a generation are: `nlen`, `e`, `H` and the length of the seed. They MUST appear in the generation record.

## 5. Seed

5.1. The seed MUST be exactly 32 bytes long.

5.2. The seed MUST come from a cryptographic random generator: that of the operating system, or a generator conforming to SP 800-90A fed by an evaluated entropy source.

5.3. The seed MUST NOT be derived from a password, a date, an identifier, a counter, or any data a third party can guess or recover.

5.4. A seed MUST serve a single generation. It MUST NOT serve a second key, even of a different size or hash function, nor any other purpose.

5.5. The seed is worth the private key: whoever holds it makes the key again. It MUST be protected at the same level as the private key, at all times.

5.6. After generation and checks, the holder MUST choose between two fates, and record the choice in their register: destroy the seed, or keep it in escrow as a means of making the key again. A destroyed seed rules out any later replay.

5.7. If the computation of section 6 fails for a seed, that seed MUST be discarded and a fresh one drawn. A discarded seed MUST NOT be altered to make it succeed.

## 6. Construction of the primes

### 6.1 The seed stream

6.1.1. The seed is read as an integer `s`, and its length in bytes is written `w` (32 for a conforming key).

6.1.2. `HashAt(j)` applies `H` to the `w` bytes that represent `(s + j) mod 2^(8w)`, then converts the digest to an integer as in 2.4.

6.1.3. `Advance(k)` replaces `s` with `s + k`, without reduction: only `HashAt` reduces modulo `2^(8w)`.

6.1.4. `Draw(k)` computes the sum, for `i` from 0 to `k`, of `HashAt(i) × 2^(i × outlen)`, then runs `Advance(k + 1)`, and returns the sum.

6.1.5. A single stream serves the whole generation, from the first call to the last. The calls MUST be made in the exact order described below: each call moves `s`, and any call added, removed or moved changes the key.

### 6.2 Small prime

6.2.1. `SmallPrime(L)` is used for `2 ≤ L ≤ 32`. It returns a prime of `L` bits and a counter.

6.2.2. Set `counter = 0`, then repeat:

1. `c = HashAt(0) ⊕ HashAt(1)`.
2. `c = 2^(L−1) + (c mod 2^(L−1))`.
3. `c = 2 × ⌊c / 2⌋ + 1`.
4. `counter = counter + 1`, then `Advance(2)`.
5. If `c` is prime, return `c` and `counter`. Primality is decided here by division: `c` is prime if no integer from 2 to `⌊√c⌋` divides it.
6. If `counter > 4 × L`, fail.

6.2.3. The bound of step 6 is strict, whereas those of 6.3 and 6.4 are not. This difference is intended: it is that of the source of the computation.

### 6.3 Provable prime

6.3.1. `ProvablePrime(L)` returns a prime of exactly `L` bits and a counter.

6.3.2. If `L ≤ 32`, return `SmallPrime(L)`.

6.3.3. Otherwise:

1. `(c0, counter) = ProvablePrime(⌈L / 2⌉ + 1)`.
2. `k = ⌈L / outlen⌉ − 1`, and `previous = counter`.
3. `x = Draw(k)`, then `x = 2^(L−1) + (x mod 2^(L−1))`.
4. `t = ⌈x / (2 × c0)⌉`.
5. Repeat:
   1. If `2 × t × c0 + 1 > 2^L`, then `t = ⌈2^(L−1) / (2 × c0)⌉`.
   2. `c = 2 × t × c0 + 1`, and `counter = counter + 1`.
   3. `a = 2 + (Draw(k) mod (c − 3))`.
   4. `z = a^(2 × t) mod c`.
   5. If `gcd(z − 1, c) = 1` and `z^c0 mod c = 1`, return `c` and `counter`.
   6. If `counter ≥ 4 × L + previous`, fail.
   7. `t = t + 1`.

6.3.4. Inside the loop, `k` keeps the value of step 2. If `z = 0`, `z − 1` is read as `c − 1`; the same holds in 6.4 with `p − 1`.

6.3.5. The test of step 5.5 is a proof: a number that passes it is prime, since `c0` is itself prime and exceeds the square root of `c`.

### 6.4 RSA prime

6.4.1. `RSAPrime(L)` returns a prime `p` of `L` bits, with `gcd(p − 1, e) = 1` and `p ≥ √2 × 2^(L−1)`.

6.4.2. Computation:

1. `(p0, _) = ProvablePrime(⌈L / 2⌉ + 1)`.
2. `k = ⌈L / outlen⌉ − 1`.
3. `B = ⌊√(2^(2L−1))⌋`, integer square root, computed exactly.
4. `x = Draw(k)`, then `x = B + (x mod (2^L − B))`.
5. `t = ⌈(2 × p0 + x) / (2 × p0)⌉`, and `counter = 0`.
6. Repeat:
   1. If `2 × (t − 1) × p0 + 1 > 2^L`, then `t = ⌈(2 × p0 + B) / (2 × p0)⌉`.
   2. `p = 2 × (t − 1) × p0 + 1`, and `counter = counter + 1`.
   3. If `gcd(p − 1, e) = 1`:
      1. `a = 2 + (Draw(k) mod (p − 3))`.
      2. `z = a^(2 × (t − 1)) mod p`.
      3. If `gcd(z − 1, p) = 1` and `z^p0 mod p = 1`, return `p`.
   4. If `counter ≥ 5 × L`, fail.
   5. `t = t + 1`.

6.4.3. At step 6.3, `Draw` is called only if `gcd(p − 1, e) = 1`. A candidate rejected by that gcd consumes nothing from the stream.

## 7. Assembling the key

7.1. Set `L = nlen / 2`, then compute in this order, on the same stream: `p = RSAPrime(L)`, then `q = RSAPrime(L)`.

7.2. While `|p − q| ≤ 2^(L − 100)`, recompute `q` by a complete call to `RSAPrime(L)` on the rest of the stream; `p` does not change.

7.3. `d = e^(−1) mod lcm(p − 1, q − 1)`. This inverse always exists, since 6.4 imposes `gcd(p − 1, e) = gcd(q − 1, e) = 1`.

7.4. If `d ≤ 2^L`, fail.

7.5. `n = p × q`. If `n` is not exactly `nlen` bits long, fail.

7.6. The private key is written in the `RSAPrivateKey` format of RFC 8017, with `dP = d mod (p − 1)`, `dQ = d mod (q − 1)` and `qInv = q^(−1) mod p`.

7.7. Any failure in sections 6 and 7 is handled as in 5.7.

## 8. Generation record

8.1. Every conforming key MUST be accompanied by a generation record, in JSON, carrying exactly the following seven fields:

| Field | Type | Content |
|---|---|---|
| `norme` | string | `GCR-0001/0.1` |
| `bits` | integer | `nlen` |
| `e` | integer | `65537` |
| `hash` | string | `sha256`, `sha384` or `sha512` |
| `graine_octets` | integer | `32` (length of the seed in bytes) |
| `n_hex` | string | the modulus in hexadecimal, lower case, without leading zero |
| `empreinte_sha256_de_n` | string | SHA-256 of the modulus, in lower-case hexadecimal |

8.2. The fingerprint is computed over the bytes of the modulus, most significant first, without a leading zero byte.

8.3. The record is public. It MUST NOT contain the seed, any part of the private key, or any field beyond those of 8.1.

## 9. Conformity levels

9.1. **Level 1, replayable key.** The key was generated according to sections 4 to 7, its record exists, and the holder has passed the checks of section 10 with the implementation that generated the key.

9.2. **Level 2, replayed key.** In addition to level 1, the checks of section 10 have been passed by an independent implementation, on a machine other than the one that generated the key.

9.3. A key of a certification authority, of a timestamping service, of a State signature, or of any function on which other keys depend MUST reach level 2 before it is put into service.

9.4. The holder declares the level reached, the date of the checks and the names of the implementations used. The replay is done under the holder's authority: the seed never leaves their custody.

## 10. Conformity checks

10.1. A verifier receives the record and the seed. It MUST run the following seven checks, in order, and stop at the first that fails:

| No. | Check |
|---|---|
| C1 | The record carries exactly the seven fields of 8.1. |
| C2 | The `norme` field names a version of this standard that the verifier knows. |
| C3 | `bits`, `e` and `hash` are allowed by section 4. |
| C4 | The seed is 32 bytes long, and `graine_octets` equals 32. |
| C5 | The declared fingerprint is that of the declared modulus. |
| C6 | The computation of sections 6 and 7, run on the seed with the parameters of the record, succeeds. |
| C7 | The modulus obtained is exactly the modulus declared. |

10.2. The verifier returns one of two verdicts: CONFORMING, or NON-CONFORMING followed by the check that failed. The reference implementation prints them in French: CONFORME and NON CONFORME.

10.3. The verifier MUST NOT display, log or transmit the seed or the private key.

## 11. Security and limits

11.1. **What the standard guarantees.** A conforming key depends only on the seed and on a public computation. No hidden choice by the maker of a piece of software or hardware can lodge in it without being revealed by a level 2 replay. The two primes are proven prime, not merely judged probable.

11.2. **What it does not guarantee.** The standard says nothing about the quality of the randomness that gave the seed. A predictable seed gives a predictable key, and replay does not reveal it. Requirement 5.2 is therefore the most important of this text, and the only one a documentary check cannot verify.

11.3. **The proof is not public.** Replay requires the seed. It convinces the holder and the auditor they admit beside them, not a remote third party.

11.4. **Observation of the computation.** The computation handles secrets. An implementation SHOULD run in constant time, or on an isolated machine that no third party observes. The reference implementation, written in Python, is not constant-time.

11.5. **Erasure.** An implementation SHOULD erase from memory the seed, `p`, `q`, `d` and all intermediate values as soon as the computation ends.

11.6. **Lifetime of RSA.** A sufficiently large quantum computer breaks RSA however the key was generated. The timetables published by several authorities plan the withdrawal of RSA during the 2030s. This standard serves while RSA serves; it does not dispense with preparing what comes next.

11.7. **Recognition of certificates.** A certificate whose key is conforming is read by all software. Whether it is *trusted* is another matter, outside this standard: it depends on the root authority being listed in the trust stores of systems and browsers, or on its recognition by the law of the country.

11.8. **Why generation.** The known large-scale failures of RSA are failures of generation, not of the algorithm: randomness disabled in a software distribution (CVE-2008-0166, 2008), primes of a recognisable form produced by a library embedded in smart cards (CVE-2017-15361, 2017). In both cases, weak keys served for a long time without their holders having any means of seeing it.

## 12. Versioning of the standard

12.1. The version appears in the `norme` field of every record. A key remains conforming to the version under which it was generated.

12.2. Any change that alters a key for the same seed gives a new major version.

12.3. Comments on this preliminary draft are received by the maintainer. Each acknowledged defect is recorded, with its correction, in the version history.

## Annex A — Test vectors

A.1. **Official vectors.** The computation of sections 6 and 7 is that of FIPS 186-4, appendix B.3.2. The vectors published by NIST for this method (ACVP-Server project, folder `gen-val/json-files/RSA-KeyGen-FIPS186-4`, file `internalProjection.json`, group 1, cases 1 to 3) apply to it: for the given seed and exponent, an implementation MUST obtain the expected `p`, `q`, `n`, `dmp1`, `dmq1` and `iqmp`.

A.2. These three cases use a 2048-bit modulus, SHA-224, a 28-byte seed and a randomly drawn exponent. These parameters fall outside section 4: they serve to test the computation, not to produce conforming keys. An implementation MUST therefore be able to run the computation with these parameters in a test mode; `outlen` is then 224 and `w` is 28.

A.3. **Vectors specific to the standard.** The file `independant/cas.json` gives five cases with the parameters of section 4 (2048, 3072 and 4096 bits; SHA-256, SHA-384, SHA-512): public test seed and expected modulus. These seeds are public: they MUST NOT serve any real key. A vector is published only once two implementations written separately have given the same key.

A.4. **Status on 18 November 2026.** Two implementations exist: the reference, in Python, and a second one, in JavaScript (`independant/gcr.js`), written from the text of this standard alone, with access neither to the first nor to FIPS 186-4. They give the same keys on the three NIST cases and on the five cases of A.3. The keys of the reference in 2048, 3072 and 4096 bits are recognised as valid by OpenSSL, which builds a root authority from them, a certificate signed by it, and verifies the chain.

A.5. This second implementation raised nine places where the text lacked precision; none made a key diverge, and all are corrected in this version. The text has not yet received outside comments.
